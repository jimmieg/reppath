PROGRESSIVE OVERLOAD — THE CORE PRINCIPLE
Progressive overload is the most important training principle after consistency.
It states that to continue improving, you must continually increase the demand
placed on your body. Without it, adaptation stops and progress plateaus.

When you consistently stress your body with exercise, it adapts so the next time
it experiences that same level of stress, it can handle it more easily. To keep
improving, you must increase the stress.

METHODS OF PROGRESSIVE OVERLOAD
There are multiple ways to apply progressive overload — not just adding weight:
- Add weight: increase load by the smallest available increment
- Add reps: perform more reps with the same weight
- Add sets: increase total volume by adding a working set
- Reduce rest: same work in less time increases density
- Improve technique: better range of motion or control at same load
- Increase frequency: train a muscle group more often per week

CHOOSING THE RIGHT METHOD BY GOAL
- Strength: prioritize load increases; keep reps low (1–5)
- Hypertrophy: use double progression (reps then weight); moderate loads
- Fat loss: prioritize volume and density; shorter rest, more total work
- Endurance: increase duration, distance, or circuit rounds

ADAPTATION — WHY THE BODY CHANGES
Exercise is a controlled stress on the body. The body adapts to maintain
homeostasis — its preferred stable internal state. Adaptations include:
- Neural: brain gets better at recruiting muscle fibers
- Muscular: muscle fibers grow larger and stronger
- Connective tissue: tendons and ligaments become more resilient
- Cardiovascular: heart becomes more efficient at delivering oxygen

FREQUENCY GUIDELINES BY EXPERIENCE
- Beginners: 3 days/week full body; respond to almost any stimulus
- Intermediate: 3–4 days/week; need more specificity to continue progressing
- Advanced: 4–6 days/week; high specificity and periodization required

DELOAD WEEKS
A deload is a planned reduction in training stress — typically 40–50% volume
reduction while maintaining intensity. It allows accumulated fatigue to dissipate
so fitness gains become visible. Recommended every 4–8 weeks depending on
training intensity and experience level.

REST AND RECOVERY
- Compound movements: minimum 48 hours before training same pattern again
- Isolation exercises: 24–48 hours
- Sleep: 7–9 hours is when the majority of muscular repair occurs
- Nutrition: adequate protein (1.6–2.2g/kg bodyweight) is required for adaptation

PERIODIZATION
Periodization is the planned variation of training variables over time.
- Linear periodization: progressive increase in load over weeks
- Undulating periodization: vary rep ranges within a week (e.g., heavy Monday,
  moderate Wednesday, light Friday)
- Block periodization: focus on one quality at a time (accumulation → intensification
  → realization)

Beginners do not need complex periodization — linear progression works until it
doesn't. Intermediate and advanced trainees benefit from planned variation.
